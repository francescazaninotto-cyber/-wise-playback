Case Study 02 · Evaluative Research · King / Candy Crush Soda, 2023–2024

Candy Crush Soda:
The Psychology of Spending

A qualitative study with 11 high and medium spenders, exploring the emotional, strategic, and contextual drivers of in-game purchases. Loss aversion, guilt, and what the game industry gets right about financial decisions under pressure.

Evaluative Qualitative · 1:1 Interviews 11 Participants Behavioural Science Monetisation Impact

The Problem

The monetisation team wanted to convert medium spenders into high spenders.

  • Candy Crush Soda is one of the most successful games of all time: a mature, optimised product
  • Business problem: how do you grow revenue on a product where core game and UX are already solved?
  • Monetisation team brief: understand what drives medium spenders, and what would move them to spend more
  • Stakeholders needed to redesign shop offers: research needed to be actionable

My role: sole researcher, full research lifecycle: from study design and recruitment through to fieldwork, analysis, and presentation to product and monetisation stakeholders.

Methodology

Why qualitative, and why these participants.

To redesign the shop, we needed to understand what was driving spending decisions for the players already buying.

Exploratory qualitative research — why interviews, not another survey

  • A prior survey had shown that spenders underreport — more survey data wouldn't help
  • Analytics has limitations: you can iterate and optimise, but you can't make innovative product changes
  • Interviews gave us the space to explore and build trust with a hard-to-recruit group
Format

Moderated 1:1 remote interviews, 60 minutes. US-based players recruited via Player Initiatives, screened on spending history (last 4 weeks and overall lifetime spend). PM and designers were invited to observe sessions throughout.

Stimulus material

Players were shown shop screenshots without prices and asked to estimate them, using a protocol adapted from behavioural economics price perception research.

Participant breakdown

Week 1

Recruitment and study preparation

Sat down with the Market Insights team to review their prior survey and identify data gaps. Screener designed and participants recruited via Player Initiatives. Discussion guide and stimulus material prepared. Built a system with the analytics team to pull full behavioural data per participant before each session.

Week 2

Fieldwork

11 moderated 1:1 remote interviews, 60 minutes each. PM and designers invited to observe. Price estimation task and shop screenshot stimulus used throughout.

Week 3

Analysis

Thematic analysis coded for motivation type, emotional valence, decision trigger, accessibility impact, and strategy vs. impulse spending.

Week 4

Triangulation and readout

Qual findings checked against behavioural data. Cross-functional readout to product and monetisation stakeholders, with recommendations ranked by impact.

After readout

Follow-up and impact

Shop and offer redesign initiated based on findings. Follow-up study commissioned to investigate low and non-spenders, and to start building player profiles segmented by spending behaviour.

The shop under study

What participants were responding to.

Participants were shown shop screenshots without prices and asked to estimate them.

Even high spenders who had been buying for over a year had no accurate sense of what they were spending. The underestimation was consistent and systematic across all groups. Because the currency is in-game, the mental connection to real money is broken.

7/11 players estimated prices significantly lower than actual.

Key Findings

Five things we learned about how players spend.

😤

1. Frustration drives every impulse purchase

100% of participants made End-of-Game Purchases (EGP) when they ran out of moves and felt stuck. The spending trigger was negative emotion, not desire.

😔

2. Guilt is universal, and suppressed by rationalisation

5/11 players mentioned guilt without being asked. The most common response was "I'm addicted to the game", framing it as a compulsion rather than a decision, which made the guilt easier to live with.

🏆

3. Achievement-linked spending feels like self-reward, not failure

7/11 players preferred offers connected to a task or milestone over standalone shop bundles. When spending came after earning something, guilt was absent. The same amount of money, in a different emotional context, produced a completely different response.

💰

4. Price perception is dramatically miscalibrated

7/11 players estimated prices as lower than actual. When shown the real prices, High Spenders focused on what they were getting ("is it worth it?"), while Medium Spenders focused on what it cost ("that's too much"). The exact same offer triggered completely different reactions depending on who was looking at it.

♿

5. Accessibility creates an overlooked spending dimension

4/11 participants had disabilities and spent under qualitatively different psychological conditions. Time-limited offers triggered anxiety rather than urgency. This was entirely absent from existing research.

Behavioural Science Framework

The psychology underneath the spending.

Findings were mapped to behavioural science frameworks to give the team a shared language for what was driving spending and where to intervene.

The green text in each card is my recommendation to the team, mapped to the underlying mechanism.

Loss Aversion

Failing one move from the end doesn't feel like failing. It feels like losing something

  • Test whether a softer prompt or alternative option changes what players do
Self-Control Failure

Saying "I'm addicted" is a way of not feeling bad about spending

  • Give players visibility of what they've spent. Most had no idea
  • Optional limits or a nudge when spending is unusually high
Endowment Effect

Once you own something in the game, it's worth more than what you paid

  • Build more features around earning and owning, not just buying in a moment
  • Season Pass, earned boosters, collections that accumulate
Affect Heuristic

Frustrated players spend differently, and they regret it more

  • When a player is clearly frustrated, don't show the buy prompt straight away
  • A free booster, a short break, or a different level might work better

So what changed

The team stopped asking "how do we get players to spend more?" and started asking "what state are they in when we give them the opportunity to spend?"

  • Spending that comes from ownership and progress: players feel good about it and come back
  • Spending that comes from frustration: players regret it and sometimes leave

Impact · Nikki Anderson Framework

Three types of research success.

1 · Strategic Influence

  • First time UX research sat at monetisation leadership planning
  • Behavioural framework adopted across the Candy Crush portfolio
  • Accessibility: formal research commission for 2025
  • Follow-on qual study on hoarding behaviours commissioned
  • Programme of regular player interviews started

2 · Direct Product Impact

  • 3 new offer strategies introduced
  • End-of-game purchase prompt redesigned
  • Season Pass elevated in offer hierarchy
  • Shared segmentation framework across Product and Monetisation

3 · Operational / ResOps Impact

  • First UX research bootcamp organised for PMs and designers
  • No-prices shop stimulus reused as standard in future monetisation studies

Reflection

What I'd do differently.

  • Treat spending as a sensitive research topic from day one
    Guilt created unexpected recruitment friction. Later rounds used more neutral language and longer recruitment windows.
  • Design for contrast, not just depth
    Adding low and non-spenders in subsequent research gave us the comparison group missing from the original study.

In summary

In four weeks, with 11 players, we got inside how and why people spend in one of the most successful mobile games in the world.

The findings changed the product, changed the strategy, and opened up research areas that hadn't existed before.
